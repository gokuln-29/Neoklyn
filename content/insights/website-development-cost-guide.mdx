Ask five agencies for a website development cost and you'll get five different numbers, usually with no explanation of why. That's not because pricing is arbitrary — it's because most quotes skip the part that actually determines the number: what you're asking them to build.

At NeoKlyn, we quote projects the same way every time, and we'd rather show the actual logic than hand over a number with no context attached.

## Why "It Depends" Is a Real Answer

"It depends" sounds like a dodge, but a website development cost genuinely can't be reduced to one figure. A five-page brochure site for a local business and a booking platform with payments, accounts, and an admin dashboard are both "a website" in casual conversation. They are not remotely the same build, and pricing them the same way would be dishonest in one direction or the other.

The useful question isn't "what does a website cost" — it's "what does *this* website cost," and that depends on a short list of concrete factors, not vibes.

## The Four Things That Actually Move the Price

In order of impact:

1. **Custom functionality vs. a template.** A site built on a page builder with a theme costs less because most of the engineering work is already done. A custom-built site — your data model, your workflows, your integrations — costs more because that work is being done for the first time, for you.
2. **Integrations.** Payments, CRMs, booking systems, inventory, third-party APIs. Each one adds real engineering time: authentication, error handling, edge cases when the third-party service is slow or down.
3. **Content and design complexity.** A handful of static pages is a different scope than a site with dozens of dynamic pages, a CMS, multiple content types, and localization.
4. **Post-launch requirements.** Ongoing updates, monitoring, and support are a separate line item from the build itself, and skipping them upfront is how "cheap" projects get expensive later.

Nothing about screen count or "how many pages" reliably predicts cost on its own — a 5-page site with a custom booking engine can cost more than a 20-page static brochure site.

## Realistic Price Bands

These are the bands we actually quote from, not a marketing range:

- **Standard websites:** from ₹50,000 (~$600) — brochure and marketing sites on a modern stack, typically delivered in 2-3 weeks.
- **Web apps and ecommerce:** ₹1-4 lakh (~$1,200-$5,000) depending on features, typically delivered in 3-6 weeks.
- **Complex platforms:** custom-scoped, typically 6-10 weeks, for multi-role systems, heavy integrations, or AI-driven features.

These numbers hold for projects built on a modern stack (we use React, Next.js, and TypeScript) rather than a page-builder plugin ecosystem — a real factor in why two "website" quotes can differ by 5x for what sounds like the same request. ## Website vs Web App: Why the Gap Is So Large

The jump from "website" to "web app" pricing isn't padding — it's a different category of engineering. A website mostly displays content. A web app manages state: user accounts, permissions, data that changes based on who's looking at it, workflows with more than one path through them.

That difference shows up directly in the price bands above. A marketing site and a booking platform with logged-in accounts and payment processing are both technically "a website," but only one of them requires a real backend, a database schema, and security review on every route that touches user data.

## Timeline vs Cost: The Trade-off Nobody Explains

Faster isn't automatically cheaper, and it isn't automatically more expensive either — it depends on which lever gets pulled. Compressing a 6-week timeline into 3 weeks by adding people has real cost implications (more parallel work, more coordination overhead). Compressing it by cutting scope changes the price for a legitimate reason: you're buying less.

The version to be skeptical of is a quote that promises a shorter timeline and a lower price for the *same* scope. Something is being cut — usually testing, review, or performance work — and it tends to surface after launch, not before.

## Red Flags in a Cheap Quote

A few patterns worth checking before signing anything:

- **No mention of hosting or maintenance costs.** A quote that only covers the build, with no clarity on what happens after launch, often front-loads a low number and back-loads the real cost.
- **Vague scope, fixed price.** If the deliverable isn't specified in enough detail to know what's included, "fixed price" doesn't mean what it sounds like — it means change requests get billed separately, and there will be change requests.
- **No testing or review step mentioned.** Speed without a review process is exactly the pattern that produces the failures described in [how we review AI-generated code](/insights/ai-code-review-checklist) — cutting review doesn't remove risk, it just moves it to after launch.
- **Performance treated as an afterthought.** A slow site costs conversions, not just engineering hours. [Google's Core Web Vitals guidance](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) is the standard reference for why load speed and interactivity directly affect whether visitors actually convert — a quote that doesn't account for this is quoting an incomplete product.

## A Quick Sanity Check Before You Sign Anything

Before committing to a number, it's worth asking the agency three direct questions: what's the total website development cost including the first year of hosting and support, what exactly is excluded from that number, and who reviews the code before launch. A team that can't answer all three clearly and quickly usually hasn't scoped the project properly yet — and a vague scope is how a reasonable-looking quote turns into a much larger bill six weeks in.
